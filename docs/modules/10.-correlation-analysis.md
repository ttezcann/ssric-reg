# [[Correlation analysis]] specifics
- Correlation analysis:
    - Examines how two continuous variables are related, especially whether they tend to increase or decrease together.
    - **Correlation is not causation**: A correlation does not show that one variable causes changes in another.
    - Correlation gives us two values:
        - **[[p-value]]**, which helps us decide whether a correlation is **statistically significant**, and
        - **[[r-value]]**, which tells us the **direction** and **strength** of the correlation.
            - The r-value (correlation coefficient) ranges from **-1** to **+1**.
<!-- slide-break -->
## [[Significance of correlation]] (with [[p-value]])
- We begin with the **[[null hypothesis]]**: The two variables have **no linear correlation in the population**.
    - We use the **[[p-value]]** to assess the evidence against this assumption.
        1. **[[Nonsignificant correlation]] (p > 0.05)**: We do not have enough evidence to conclude that the variables are correlated in the population.
            1. ![Figure showing points scattered without a clear trend.](../assets/attachments/modules/10.-correlation-analysis/nonsignificant-correlation.png){width="600"}
        2. **[[Significant correlation]] (p < 0.05)**: We have evidence against our assumption that there is no linear correlation in the population.
            - We then use the **[[r-value]]** to describe the direction and strength of the correlation.
<!-- slide-break -->
## [[Direction of correlation]] (with [[r-value]])
- We use the **r-value** to determine the direction of a correlation.
    - When the r-value is **positive** (for example, `r = 0.250`), we have a:
        - **[[Positive correlation]]**: As one variable increases, the other variable **tends to increase** as well.
    - When the r-value is **negative** (for example, `r = -0.250`), we have a:
        - **[[Negative correlation]]**: As one variable increases, the other variable **tends to decrease**.
            - ![Two scatterplots illustrate types of correlation: positive correlation shows points trending upward from left to right, negative correlation shows points trending downward.](../assets/attachments/modules/10.-correlation-analysis/positive-negative-correlation.png){width="900"}
<!-- slide-break -->
## [[Strength of correlation]] (with [[r-value]])
- We use the absolute value of the **[[r-value]]** written as **|r|**, to describe the strength of a correlation. The vertical bars mean that we ignore whether the r-value is positive or negative. In this course, we use these guidelines:
    - **|r| < 0.3** ➜ [[weak correlation]]
        - r = 0.251 and r = **-**0.251 are both weak correlations.
    - **0.3 ≤ |r| < 0.5** ➜ [[moderate correlation]]
        - r = 0.426 and r = **-**0.426 are both moderate correlations.
    - **|r| ≥ 0.5** ➜ [[strong correlation]]
        - r = 0.785 and r = **-**0.785 are both strong correlations.
            - ![Grid of six example scatterplots showing positive and negative correlations at weak, moderate, and strong levels. The top row shows positive correlations and the bottom row shows negative correlations, with point patterns becoming tighter around the trend line as the relationship gets stronger.](../assets/attachments/modules/10.-correlation-analysis/direction-strength-correlation.png){width="900"}
        - !!! info "Use the size of the r-value to compare strength, regardless of whether it is positive or negative."
            - **r = -0.673** is stronger than **r = 0.567** because 0.673 is greater than 0.567.
            - **r = -0.432** is stronger than **r = 0.322** because 0.432 is greater than 0.322.
            - **r = 0.567** is stronger than **r = -0.322** because 0.567 is greater than 0.322.
                - The **minus sign** tells us the **direction**, not how strong the correlation is.

# [[Confounding variable]]
- A **confounding variable** is a third variable that helps explain the relationship between the two variables we are studying. Here, **temperature** affects both **frequency of sunburn** and **ice cream sales**.
    - Sunburns and ice cream sales may increase together because both are related to **temperature**, not because one causes the other.
        - ![A simple diagram shows a thermometer at the top with arrows pointing to an ice cream icon on one side and a running person on the other, indicating that temperature influences both ice cream consumption and outdoor activity.](../assets/attachments/modules/10.-correlation-analysis/confounding-variable.png){width="400"}
    - **Other confounding variable examples**:
        - Number of schools in a city and number of crimes in a city
            - **Confounding Variable:** City population
        - Shoe size and reading ability in children
            - **Confounding Variable:** Age of the child
        - Outdoor exercise frequency and vitamin D levels
            - **Confounding Variable:** Sunlight exposure

# [[Bivariate correlation]]
- Shows the relationship between two continuous variables in a table-graph format:
    - [[Correlation table]] shows this relationship in a table format:
        - Reports the correlation coefficient (r-value) and the p-value used to assess statistical significance.
    - [[Scatterplot graph]] shows this relationship in a graph format:
        - In addition to the r-value and p-value, displays the shape of the relationship.

### [[Correlation table]] and [[scatterplot graph]] #interpretation
- !!! note "Significant (p < 0.05) and weak (|r| < 0.3) correlation interpretation sample"
    There is a **statistically significant correlation** between **respondents' education in years** and **television screen time in hours** since the **p-value is less than 0.05**.

    This correlation is **negative and weak** since the **r-value is -0.163**.

    This means that as **respondents' education in years** increases, **television screen time in hours** tends to decrease.
- !!! quote "Significant (p < 0.05) and negative correlation interpretation template"
    - There is a **statistically significant correlation** between **[[variable label]] 1** and **variable label 2** since the **p-value is less than 0.05**.
    - This correlation is **negative and weak** since the **r-value is -0.xxx**.
        - **OR** This correlation is **negative and moderate** since the **r-value is -0.xxx** (if 0.3 ≤ |r| < 0.5).
        - **OR** This correlation is **negative and strong** since the **r-value is -0.xxx** (if |r| ≥ 0.5).
    - This means that as **variable label 1** increases, **variable label 2** tends to decrease.
- !!! success "Interpretation explanation"
    - We first check the **[[p-value]]**.
        - When the p-value is less than 0.05, the correlation is **statistically significant**.
            - This example has a **statistically significant correlation** since **p < 0.05**.
    - We then check the **[[r-value]]**.
        - **Direction:** The minus sign (-) means a **[[negative correlation]]**.
            - Here, **r = -0.163**, so the correlation is negative.
        - **Strength:** We use the size of the r-value without its minus sign. Check the **[[strength of correlation]]** guideline.
            - Here, **|r| = 0.163**, which is less than 0.3, so this is a **[[weak correlation]]**.
<!-- slide-break -->
- !!! tip "Further considerations"
    - **Shape of the [[scatterplot graph]]:** The line stays near 4 hours from 0 to about 11 years of education, then drops steadily after 12 years to about 2.3 hours at 20 years. The decline happens mainly after high school.
    - **Connecting to a sociological concept:** **Cultural capital** suggests that schooling builds tastes for activities other than TV. The significant negative correlation (**r = -0.163, p < 0.001**) fits this idea, but it is weak, and the flat part of the line suggests it applies mainly to education beyond high school.
    - **Responsible reporting:** Describing this weak correlation as strong could lead readers to stereotype less-educated people as lazy, when education explains only a small part of TV time.
    - **Who is missing:** `tvhours` asks only about television, so time spent streaming on phones or laptops is not captured. The result describes TV watching, not all screen time.

# [[Multivariate correlation]]
- Shows the relationships among multiple (more than two) continuous variables in a table-graph format.
    - A correlation matrix examines each **pair of variables** separately.
    - [[Correlation table matrix]] shows these relationships in a table format:

    - [[Scatterplot graph matrix]] shows these relationships in a graph format:
        - In addition to the r-value and p-value, displays the shape of the relationship.
