# Chi-square basics
- !!! note "Relationship between preferred pet and respondents’ age group"
    - Do you think there is a relationship between **preferred pet** and respondents’ **age group**?  
    - In other words, do you think the **preferred pet** is influenced by respondents’ **age group**?
        - | Age group |          Cat |          Dog |        Total
        |-----------|-------------:|-------------:|------------:
        | Younger   | 207<br>41.4% | 293<br>58.6% |  500<br>100%
        | Older     | 267<br>53.4% | 233<br>46.6% |  500<br>100%
        | Total     | 474<br>47.4% | 526<br>52.6% | 1000<br>100%
            - ??? tip "Show the answer"
                - While the table shows differences in preferences between age groups (e.g., younger people prefer dogs more than older people), we **CANNOT** conclude that the differences are statistically significant. 
                    - To assess the evidence against independence between age group and preferred pet, we use a statistical test like the chi-square test, assuming its requirements are met.
<!-- slide-break -->
- The [[chi-square]] test is used to discover if there is a relationship between:
    - Two [[categorical]] variables.
    - The chi-square test can only compare categorical variables.
        - It cannot make comparisons between continuous variables or between categorical and continuous variables.
<!-- slide-break -->
# Functions of variables: Factor variable and outcome variable
- A [[variable]]:
    - Depending on the [[functions of the variable]] in the analysis is either:
        - [[Outcome variable]]: the main topic that we investigate. It is the outcome: What is being affected or changed? This is also called the *dependent variable*.
        - [[Factor variable]]: the variable used to explain or understand the outcome variable (main topic). This is also called the *independent variable*.
            - A factor variable is assumed to cause a change in the outcome variable.
<!-- slide-break -->
- If we consider the "relationship between preferred pet and respondents’ age group" table, we need to ask the following question:
    - In this table, what seems to change what?
        - Someone's preferred pet will not change their age group. However, the opposite could be the case: someone's age group *may* influence their preferred pet. Therefore:
            - Age group is the [[factor variable]],
            - Preferred pet is the [[outcome variable]].
<!-- slide-break -->
- We need to identify, *logically*, which variable plays which role, because the codes we use depend on this distinction.
    - Some variables can function as either a [[factor variable]] or an [[outcome variable]]:
        - For example, we could argue that the level of education increases someone’s income. In this case; 
            - **Education** is the **factor variable**, and
            - **Income** is the **outcome variable**.
        - In another study, we could argue the opposite: income increases someone’s level of education. In this case, 
            - **Income** is the **factor variable**, and 
            - **Education** is the **outcome variable**.
        - These two research examples are completely different because their factor and outcome variables are defined differently.
<!-- slide-break -->
- However, some variables cannot *realistically* be outcome variables:
    - Nothing can change a person’s age.
    - Similarly, variables such as place of birth or ethnicity cannot be outcomes because they are not influenced by other variables in the dataset.
    - These variables can only serve as factor variables because they may influence other outcomes:
        - **Age** → may influence political attitudes, health status, or technology use.
        - **Place of birth** → may influence language ability or cultural preferences.
        - **Ethnicity** → may influence income, occupation, or health outcomes.
<!-- slide-break -->
# Null hypothesis
- Before we examine whether two variables are related, we start with an assumption that they are **not related in the population**.
    - This assumption is called the **[[null hypothesis]]**.
        - For example, if we want to study the relationship between **age group** and **preferred pet**, our null hypothesis is that **age group and preferred pet are not related in the population**.
        - We then use data from our sample to see whether we have evidence against this assumption.
            - This is simply our starting assumption. We do not yet know whether the two variables are related.
<!-- slide-break -->
# Statistical significance and p-value
- In this and the following modules, we will use **[[statistical significance]]** to help us understand whether two variables are related. For example, is **age group** related to **preferred pet**?
    - We begin by assuming that the two variables are **not related in the population**. This assumption is called the **[[null hypothesis]]**.
        - We use the **[[p-value]]** to assess the evidence against this assumption.
            - A **smaller p-value** gives us more reason to question our assumption that the two variables are not related.
                - All p-values are between **0** and **1**.
                    - In this course, when a **p-value is less than 0.05**, we call the result **statistically significant**. This provides evidence against our assumption that the variables are not related.
                        - We write this as **p < 0.05**.
<!-- slide-break -->
## How to check statistical significance using the [[p-value]]?
- **[[Is my p-value less than 0.05?]]**
    - To determine [[statistical significance]]
        - We can use this website: [www.whichnumberislarger.com](https://www.whichnumberislarger.com/){: target="_blank" rel="noopener" }.
            - Enter your p-value in the first box and 0.05 in the second box.
                - If your p-value is less than 0.05, the result is statistically significant.
                - If your p-value is 0.05 or greater, the result is not statistically significant under this course's p < 0.05 rule.
- **[[Check asterisks]]**
    - Another way to determine [[statistical significance]]:
        - Our tables and figures use asterisks (*) to indicate statistical significance.
        - If you see **at least one asterisk (*)**, the result is statistically significant at the 0.05 level.
            - !!! note "The number of asterisks (*) indicates the significance level"
                - **(1)** * means p < 0.05  
                - **(2)** ** means p < 0.01  
                - **(3)** *** means p < 0.001  
<!-- slide-break -->
# [[Chi-square]] specifics
- When both [[factor variable]] and [[outcome variable]] are [[categorical]], we conduct chi-square.
    - For example, we could wonder if income group has a statistically significant association with life satisfaction level:
        - **Factor variable**: Income groups [(1) Low, (2) Medium, (3)High],
        - **Outcome variable**: Life satisfaction level [(1)Not satisfied, (2) Moderately satisfied, (3) Satisfied],
            - If the p-value that the chi-square test generates is **less than 0.05**, the test provides evidence of an association between income group and life satisfaction level in the population.

## [[Chi-square]] #interpretation significant (p < 0.05)
- !!! note "Significant (p < 0.05) chi-square interpretation sample"
    There is a statistically significant association between **respondents' education degree** and **perceived personal health quality** since the **p-value is less than 0.05**.

    We can conclude that **perceived personal health quality** differs **in a statistically significant way** across respondents with **less than high school**, **high school**, **associate/junior college**, **bachelor's**, and **graduate degree**.

- !!! quote "Significant (p < 0.05) chi-square interpretation template"
    There is a statistically significant association between **[[variable label]]** **(of [[factor variable]])** and **[[variable label]]** **(of [[outcome variable]])** since the **[[p-value]] is less than 0.05**.

    We can conclude that **[[variable label]]** **(of [[outcome variable]])** differs **in a statistically significant way** across **[[value label]] 1**, **[[value label]] 2**, ... **(of [[factor variable]])**.

- !!! success "Interpretation explanation"
    - The **[[p-value]]** tells us whether the relationship between the two variables is statistically significant.
        - When the p-value is less than 0.05, we conclude that there is a **statistically significant association** between the two variables.
        - When the p-value is 0.05 or greater, we do not have enough evidence to conclude that the two variables are related.
    - **(1) First sentence**: We use the **variable labels** of the **factor variable** and **outcome variable** to describe their association.
        - "There is a statistically significant association between **respondents' education degree** and **perceived personal health quality** since the **p-value is less than 0.05**."
    - **(2) Second sentence**: We explain what the significant chi-square result means.
        - We list the value labels of the **factor variable** and compare them on the **outcome variable**.
            - For example: "We can conclude that **perceived personal health quality** differs **in a statistically significant way** across respondents with **less than high school**, **high school**, **associate/junior college**, **bachelor's**, and **graduate degree**."
