# T-test analysis basics
- !!! abstract "Group I and Group II"
    - Is there a **significant** income **difference** between Group I and Group II?
        - |Group I <br><br> (Income mean)| Group II <br><br> (Income mean) |
          |---|---:|
          |  $32,000 | $32,000|
            - ??? tip "Show the answer"
                - We can’t know the answer without seeing the distribution of the variable.  
                - The mean of the two groups look same, however, there are more deviations in Group II.  
                - RStudio will show whether there is a significant difference. Relying solely on mean scores can be misleading!
                    - | id | Group I | Group II |
                      |---:|---:|---:|
                      | 1 | $32,000 | $23,150 |
                      | 2 | $31,000 | **120,300** |
                      | 3 | $29,500 | $12,100 |
                      | 4 | $30,100 | $32,000 |
                      | 5 | $32,500 | $25,000 |
                      | 6 | $31,150 | $20,000 |
                      | 7 | $30,230 | **$11,000** |
                      | 8 | $32,400 | $12,200 |
                      | 9 | $33,000 | **$11,100** |
                      | 10 | $38,120 | $53,150 |
<!-- slide-break -->
- The [[t-test]] is used to determine if:
    -  There is a significant difference ([[statistical significance]]) between the means (average scores) of two groups. 
    -  We conduct t-test, when we want to compare two means (the scores must be continuous).
        - We'll check the [[p-value]].
            - :material-lightbulb: [[Is my p-value less than 0.05?]]
        -  T-test can answer following questions:
            - Whether **the average number of close friends** reported by **married individuals** versus **single individuals** significantly differs.
            - Whether **the average hours of daily internet usage** between **younger (18–34)** and **older (65+)** age cohorts significantly differs.
            - Whether **the average income** between **male** and **female** full-time workers significantly differs.
<!-- slide-break -->
- For example, we would use a t-test if we wished to compare the reading achievement scores of juniors and seniors. 
    - | id | Class standing <br/> (1 = juniors; 2 = seniors) | Reading achievement score <br/> (0 to 100) |
      |:--:|:---------------:|:-------------:|
      | 1 | 2 | 88 |
      | 2 | 2 | 79 |
      | 3 | 1 | 54 |
      | 4 | 1 | 22 |
      | 5 | 1 | 91 |
      | ... | ... | ... |
      | 300 | 2 | 78 |
<!-- slide-break -->
    - Reading achievement mean score of **juniors**: **61.20**
    - Reading achievement mean score of **seniors**: **83.40**
        - The difference is 22.20 points.
    - The t-test analysis below shows if this score difference is statistically significant.
        - See the "Difference" column: 22.20. On average, the reading achievement score of Seniors' is 22.20 points higher than the Juniors'. 
            - Now check the p-value column. Based on the t-test analysis, this 22.20 points difference is statistically significant because the p-value is less than 0.05.
    - | Outcome variable          | Class = Junior | Class = Senior | Difference | 95% CI           | t(298) |      p       |
      |---------------------------|:--------------:|:--------------:|:----------:|:-----------------|:------:|:------------:|
      | Reading achievement score |     61.20      |     83.40      | ==-22.20== | (-26.80, -17.60) | -9.51  | ==0.000***== |
<!-- slide-break -->
# T-test specifics
-  In [[t-test]], 
    -  [[Factor variable]] should be:
        -  [[Binary]]; 
    -  [[Outcome variable]] should be:
        -  [[Continuous]].
    -  In the example above, class standing is the factor variable with two values: 1: Junior; 2: Senior, thus binary.
        -  Reading achievement score is the outcome variable ranged from 0 to 100, thus continuous.
            -  The t-test table shows that students' class standing (being junior or senior) has a significant effect on their reading achievement score (p<0.05).
                - ```mermaid
                flowchart LR
                    subgraph F["Factor variable (Binary)"]
                        A[Class standing<br/>1=Juniors; 2=Seniors]
                    end
            
                    subgraph O["Outcome variable (Continuous)"]
                        B[Reading achievement score<br/>Range: 0-100]
                    end
            
                    A ==>|Has an effect on <br/> Significant effect; p<0.05| B
                ```
                    - The opposite couldn't be the case. Just because a student's reading achievement score is high, this wouldn't make them a senior next day. 
                        - That's why, class standing is the factor, and the reading achievement score is the outcome.

- **[[Model code]]**
    - ```r linenums="1"  hl_lines="1"

            - [[Outcome variable]] first, [[factor variable]] second.

|-----------|:-------------:|:---------------:|:-----------:|:--------|:-----------:|:--:|

<!-- slide-break -->
## [[T-test]] #interpretation significant (p < 0.05)
- !!! quote "Significant (p < 0.05) t-test interpretation sample"
    The average **personal income** of **males** is $**43,037**, The average **personal income** of **females** is $**31,301**.

    **Personal income** differs by **respondents’ sex** in a statistically significant way since the p-value is less than 0.05.
- !!! note "Significant (p < 0.05) t-test interpretation template"
    The average **[[variable label]]** of **[[outcome variable]]** of **[[value label]] 1** of **[[factor variable]]** is **mean** year/dollar/point/score, while **variable label** of **outcome variable** of **value label 1** of **factor variable** is **mean** year/dollar/point/score.

    **Variable label** of **outcome variable** differs by **variable label** of **factor variable** in a statistically significant way since the p-value is less than 0.05.
- !!! success "Interpretation explanation"
    - The [[p-value]] tells us whether the difference between the two groups is statistically significant.
        - When the p-value is less than 0.05, we conclude that the two groups have different averages on the outcome variable.
        - When the p-value is greater than 0.05, we conclude that the two groups have similar averages on the outcome variable.
    - **First sentence**: We report the average of the **outcome variable** for each value of the **factor variable**.
        - For example: "The average personal income of males is $43,037, while the average personal income of females is $31,301."
    - **Second sentence**: We explain what the significant t-test result means.
        - Since this is a significant result, we say the outcome variable **differs by** the factor variable in a statistically significant way.

## Find the variables in Variables in GSS page
1. We want to make sure that the factor variable, `marital`, is binary, and the outcome variable, `tvhours`, is continuous variable.
2. [[Search]] the variable names, `marital` and `tvhours`, in [Variables in GSS](../resources/variables-in-gss.md) page.
    - | Variable name | Variable label | Variable type | Question wording and response categories |
      |---|---|---|---|
      | `marital` | Respondents' marital status | Nominal | Are you currently — married, widowed, divorced, separated, or have you never been married?<br><br>*(1: Married; 2: Widowed; 3: Divorced; 4: Separated; 5: Never married)* |
      | `tvhours` | Television screen time in hours | Continuous | On the average day, how many hours do you personally watch television? |
3. The original `marital` variable is not binary, but nominal. 
    1. That's why we first need to recode `marital` and make it a binary variable.
<!-- slide-break -->
## [[Merging values]] #code
- **[[Model code]]**
    - ```r linenums="1"  hl_lines="1 2 3 4 5"
    gss$new_variable_here <-
    rec(gss$original_variable_here, rec =
    "number1, number2 = 1 [label1]; 
    number3, number4= 2 [label2]",
    var.label = "Recoded variable label")
    ```
- **[[Working code]]**
    - ```r linenums="1"  hl_lines="1 2 3 4 5"
    gss$maritalbinary <- 
    rec(gss$marital, rec = 
    "5 = 1 [Single]; 
    1, 2, 3, 4 = 2 [Nonsingle]",
    var.label = "Recoded respondents' marital status")
    ```
        - **Line 1:** We put the new variable name for the new recoded variable here, `maritalbinary`. 
            - We’ll type this name. No space, no special characters. Add “groups”, “reversed”, or “recoded” at the end of the original variable name or type anything that you will remember what this variable is.
        - **Line 2:** We put the original variable we want to recode here, `marital`. The new variable will be created based on the original variable's values.
        - **Lines 3-4** We merge values in these lines. "[...]" are the new labels for the new values. These will appear on our outputs:
            - [Single] and [Nonsingle]
        - **Line 6:** We write this new variable's variable label here "`Recoded respondents' marital status`".
            - [[Find this working code in the R script file]].
                - [[Highlighting and running]] this code will create a new variable, `maritalgroups`, and will be added to GSS dataset.
