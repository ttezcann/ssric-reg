# Logistic regression structure
- [[Logistic regression]] is a regression model where; 
    - The [[outcome variable]] is 
        - A [[dummy variable]], and thus [[binary]], which can take only two values, "1" and "0", such as, 
            - pass **(1)** / fail **(0)**, 
            - win **(1)** / lose **(0)**, 
            - alive **(1)** / dead **(0)**,
            - healthy **(1)** / sick **(0)**,
            - return **(1)** / stay **(0)**,
            - voted **(1)** / didn’t vote **(0)**.
        - Categorical factor variables can also be represented by dummy variables, as in the previous module.
- We examine how factor variables are associated with the **odds** of the outcome coded **1**.
    - For example, we can examine whether hours of studying (continuous factor variable; min: 0, max: 7) is associated with the odds of passing an exam (binary outcome variable; 1: passing; 0: failing).
        - ```mermaid
        flowchart LR
        subgraph F["Continuous variable"]
            A[Hours of studying<br><br>min: 0, max: 7]
        end

        subgraph O["Dummy outcome variable"]
            B[Passing the exam <br><br> 1: passing; 0: failing]
        end

        A -.->|Association?| B
        ```

- The figure below shows that the **odds ratio of passing the exam increases** with study hours, using **2 hours of studying as the comparison**.
    - At **3 hours**, the odds ratio is **4.20**.
    - At **4 hours**, the odds ratio is **17.64**.
    - At **5 hours**, the odds ratio is **74.09**.
        - This means a student who studies for 5 hours **has 74.09 times the odds of passing the exam** compared with a student who studies for 2 hours.

# Logistic regression specifics
- [[Logistic regression]] table provides:
    - [[Odds ratio]] (OR): Shows how the odds of the outcome coded **1** compare for a one-unit increase in a factor variable, while the other factor variables remain the same.
        - For example, one additional hour of studying is associated with **4.20 times the odds** of passing the exam.
        - An odds ratio **greater than 1** means **higher estimated odds** of the outcome.
        - An odds ratio **less than 1** means **lower estimated odds** of the outcome.
        - An odds ratio **equal to 1** means **no estimated difference in odds**. An odds ratio does not directly describe changes in probability.
        - [[Standard error]]: Shows the uncertainty in an estimated coefficient. Smaller standard errors generally mean more precise estimates. Standard errors appear in parentheses below the odds ratios.
    - [[Standardized odds ratio]] (std. OR): An odds ratio rescaled to help compare associations between factor variables measured in different units.
        - This is useful because different variables are measured differently.
            - Age is measured in years.
            - Education is measured in years.
            - Income may be measured in dollars.
            - Occupational prestige may be measured with a score.
    - [[p-value]]: Helps us assess evidence against the starting assumption that a factor variable has no association with the odds of the outcome (an odds ratio of **1**) under the model's assumptions.
        - When the p-value is less than 0.05, we call the association **statistically significant**.
        - When the p-value is greater than 0.05, we do not have enough evidence to conclude that the factor variable has a statistically significant association with the odds of the outcome.
    - [[Tjur R-squared]]: Tjur R-squared tells us how well a logistic regression model distinguishes between the two groups of the outcome variable.
        - A larger value means the model distinguishes between the groups more clearly.
            - We report this difference in **percentage points**.

- !!! success "Interpretation explanation"
    - **First section: The significance levels**
        - We identify the **variable labels** of factor variables that have statistically significant associations with the odds of the **outcome variable**.
            - When the p-value is less than 0.05,, we say the factor variable has a **statistically significant association** with the odds of the outcome.
            - When the p-value is greater than 0.05 (p > 0.05), we say the factor variable is **not a statistically significant factor** of the outcome.
    - **Second section: The explanation of odds ratios**
        - We use the **Odds Ratios** column to explain the associations of statistically significant factor variables with the odds of the outcome coded **1**.
            - For a numerical factor variable, we explain the odds ratio for a one-unit increase (year, point, score, etc.).
            - For a dummy variable, we compare the included group with the **omitted comparison group**.
            - When the odds ratio is **greater than 1**, use the odds ratio and write **times higher odds**.
            - When the odds ratio is **less than 1**, divide **1 by the odds ratio** and write **times lower odds**.
    - **Third section: The explanation of standardized odds ratios**
        - We use the **std. OR** column to compare the relative size of the standardized associations with the odds of the outcome.
            - When the standardized odds ratio is **less than 1**, divide **1 by the standardized odds ratio**.
                - **Note:** We do not include nonsignificant factor variables in this section.
    - **Fourth section: The explanation of Tjur R-squared**
        - We report **Tjur R-squared** as a **difference in percentage points** between the average predicted probabilities of the two groups.
            - We list **all factor variables included in the model**, including those that are not statistically significant.
            - **Note:** Tjur R-squared is **not** the percentage of variation explained by a logistic regression model.
