# [[Regression]] definition
- Regression is a statistical technique used to model relationships between variables.
    - In regression, we examine how [[factor variable]]s are related to the [[outcome variable]].
    - Like [[correlation analysis]], regression does not by itself establish cause and effect. In regression, we specify which variable is the outcome and which variables are the factors. Here is a correlation table as an example:
<!-- slide-break -->
    - ```r
    tab_corr (gss[c("age", "childs")],  
    p.numeric = T, triangle="lower")
    ```
    - |                                     |    *Respondents' age*     | *Number of children respondents* have |
    |---------------------------------------|:-------------------------:|:------------------------------------:|
    | *Respondents' age*                    |                           |                                       |
    | *Number of children respondents have* | r = 0.297<br>p < 0.001*** |                                       |

    - !!! note "Significant (p < 0.05) and weak (|r| < 0.3) correlation interpretation sample"
        There is a **statistically significant correlation** between **respondents' age** and **number of children respondents have** since the **p-value is less than 0.05**.

        This correlation is **positive and weak** since the **r-value is 0.297**.
        
        This means that as **respondents' age** increases, **the number of children respondents have** tends to increase.
    - In regression analysis, respondents' age is the factor variable, and the number of children is the outcome variable.
        - Having more children does not make a respondent older. That is why we use age to help explain differences in the number of children, rather than treating the number of children as a factor that determines age.
            - Otherwise, we might argue that having children increases respondents' age, or not having children magically decreases respondent's age.

# [[Types of regression modeling based on outcome variable]]
- There are two types of regression modeling based on outcome variable:
    1. **[[Linear regression]]**
        - The [[outcome variable]] is
            - [[Continuous]].
        - There can be one or more [[factor variable]]s.
    2. **[[Logistic regression]]**
        - The [[outcome variable]] is
            - [[Binary]] (coded with two values, which we will discuss later).
        - There can be one or more [[factor variable]]s.
<!-- slide-break -->
# [[Types of regression modeling based on modeling type]]
- Regression models can be used for three different purposes. A model can have more than one purpose:
    1. **[[Explanatory modeling]]**
        - These models use existing research and theory to choose factors that may help explain the outcome variable.
            - They are theory-driven.
            - An explanatory regression model can test relationships suggested by a theory.
    2. **[[Predictive modeling]]**
        - The goal is to predict the outcome variable using the values of the factor variables. For example, we might use several variables to predict personal income.
            - Predictive models focus on how well the model predicts an outcome.
    3. **[[Descriptive modeling]]**
        - Descriptive models summarize the relationships found in the data without claiming that one variable causes another.
            - We select variables to explore patterns in the data.
            - These models describe patterns, even when our main goal is not prediction or a causal explanation.
    - We will see some examples of regression analysis and outputs in the following section.

## Example 1: Hospitalization days
- [[Predictive modeling]] and [[explanatory modeling]] can be used to study hospital stays. For example, we can predict how long a patient may stay and examine which patient characteristics are associated with longer or shorter stays.
    - Imagine working in a hospital, calculating hospitalization days to arrange available beds for the next month.
    - A regression model might predict hospitalization days using the following variables, among many others:
        - **Seriousness of the operation**: A continuous variable that quantifies the complexity and inherent risks of the operation on a scale (0: not serious; 100: very serious).
        - **Age**: The age of the patient in years.
        - **Healthy lifestyle**: A continuous variable that provides a measure of health lifestyle (0: extremely poor; 100: extremely healthy).
        - **Previous hospitalizations**: The total number of days spent in the hospital in the past due to medical procedures or illnesses.
<!-- slide-break -->
- Here's what such regression analysis table looks like:
- **Hospitalization days**
    - | *Factors*                    | *Coeff.*        | *std. Coeff.*   | *p*                 |
      | ---------------------------- | :-------------: | :------------: | :-----------------: |
      | (Intercept)                  | -2.50<br>(0.85) | 0.00<br>(0.01) | **0.003**\*\*       |
      | Seriousness of the operation | 0.30<br>(0.01)  | 0.35<br>(0.01) | **< 0.001**\*\*\*    |
      | Age                          | 0.12<br>(0.01)  | 0.18<br>(0.01) | **< 0.001**\*\*\*    |
      | Healthy lifestyle            | -0.22<br>(0.01) | -0.23<br>(0.01)| **< 0.001**\*\*\*    |
      | Previous hospitalizations    | 0.25<br>(0.01)  | 0.32<br>(0.01) | **< 0.001**\*\*\*    |
      | Observations                 | 7995            |                |                     |
      | R² / R² adjusted             | 0.65 / 0.65     |                |                     |

<!-- slide-break -->
- **Formula:**
    - Predicted hospitalization days = -2.50 + (0.30 × seriousness of the operation) + (0.12 × age) + (-0.22 × healthy lifestyle) + (0.25 × previous hospitalizations)
    - The formula starts at **-2.50 days** (the intercept, which applies when all factors equal zero). Each factor then changes the predicted number of days. The intercept is not a realistic hospital stay for this example.
- **Example calculation:**
    - Say a patient has:
    - Seriousness of the operation = 40, Age = 88, Healthy lifestyle = 54, Previous hospitalizations = 6
        - Plug each value into the formula:
- **Formula table**
    - | Step                                    | Calculation | Result           |
    | --------------------------------------- | ----------- | ---------------- |
    | Start with the baseline                 |             | -2.50 days       |
    | Seriousness of the operation (0.30 × 40) | adds        | +12 days         |
    | Age (0.12 × 88)                         | adds        | +10.56 days      |
    | Healthy lifestyle (-0.22 × 54)          | subtracts   | -11.88 days      |
    | Previous hospitalizations (0.25 × 6)    | adds        | +1.50 days          |
    | **Predicted hospitalization days**      |             | **= 9.68 days** |
<!-- slide-break -->
- Each factor shifts the predicted hospitalization days up or down:
    - Each one-point increase in **seriousness of the operation** is associated with **0.30 more predicted days**.
    - Each additional year of **age** is associated with **0.12 more predicted days**.
    - Each one-point increase in **healthy lifestyle** is associated with **0.22 fewer predicted days**.
    - Each additional day of **previous hospitalizations** is associated with **0.25 more predicted days**.
- !!! warning 
    - In practice, this calculation is done for all **7,995** patients at once; not manually one by one. 
        - This example shows how to calculate predictions from coefficients.

## Example 2: Car insurance rates
- [[Predictive modeling]] and [[explanatory modeling]] can be used to study car insurance rates. We can predict rates from city characteristics and examine which characteristics are associated with higher or lower rates.
    - Why do car insurance rates vary between different states and even between cities within the same state?
- A regression model might predict car insurance rates using the following variables, among many others:
    - **Population of the city**: Larger populations may lead to higher accident rates due to increased traffic.
    - **Accidents per 1000 people**: Reflects the overall risk associated with the city.
    - **Quality of roads**: A city-wide average rating of road conditions.
    - **Crime rate**: Higher crime rates, especially theft and vandalism, lead to higher insurance premiums.
    - **Weather patterns**: Cities with severe weather conditions could experience more weather-related accidents (0:bad, 100:ideal).
    - **Traffic congestion levels**: Cities with higher levels of congestion experience more fender benders.
    - **Average commute time**: Longer commutes can increase the probability of accidents occurring.

- **Car insurance rate**

      |---------------------------|:----------------------:|:--------------:|:-------------------:|
      | (Intercept)               | 1452.87<br>(50.00)     | 0.00<br>(0.01) | **< 0.001**\*\*\*    |
      | Population of the city    | 0.0015<br>(0.000019)   | 0.41<br>(0.01) | **< 0.001**\*\*\*    |
      | Accidents per 1000 people | 2.35<br>(0.05)         | 0.24<br>(0.01) | **< 0.001**\*\*\*    |
      | Quality of roads          | -1.78<br>(0.04)        | -0.21<br>(0.01)| **< 0.001**\*\*\*    |
      | Crime rate                | 0.97<br>(0.03)         | 0.17<br>(0.01) | **< 0.001**\*\*\*    |
      | Weather patterns          | -0.045<br>(0.00198)    | -0.12<br>(0.01)| **< 0.001**\*\*\*    |
      | Traffic congestion levels | 0.215<br>(0.0067)      | 0.17<br>(0.01) | **< 0.001**\*\*\*    |
      | Average commute time      | 0.28<br>(0.01)         | 0.16<br>(0.01) | **< 0.001**\*\*\*    |
      | Observations              | 19495                  |                |                     |
      | R² / R² adjusted          | 0.45 / 0.45            |                |                     |

<!-- slide-break -->
- **Formula:**
    - Predicted rate = 1452.87 + (0.0015 × population) + (2.35 × accidents per 1000) + (-1.78 × road quality) + (0.97 × crime rate) + (-0.045 × weather patterns) + (0.215 × traffic congestion) + (0.28 × average commute time)
    - The formula starts at $**1,452.87** (the intercept, which applies when all factors equal zero). Each city characteristic then changes the predicted rate.
- **Example calculation:**
    - Say a city has:
    - Population = 500,000, Accidents per 1000 = 8, Road quality = 60, Crime rate = 12, Weather patterns = 45, Traffic congestion = 7, Average commute time = 30
        - Plug each value into the formula:
- **Formula table**
    -   | Step                             | Calculation | Result          |
        | -------------------------------- | ----------- | --------------- |
        | Start with the baseline          |             | $1,452.87       |
        | Population (0.0015 × 500,000)    | adds        | +$750.00        |
        | Accidents per 1000 (2.35 × 8)    | adds        | +$18.80         |
        | Road quality (-1.78 × 60)        | subtracts   | -$106.80        |
        | Crime rate (0.97 × 12)           | adds        | +$11.64         |
        | Weather patterns (-0.045 × 45)   | subtracts   | -$2.03          |
        | Traffic congestion (0.215 × 7)   | adds        | +$1.51          |
        | Average commute time (0.28 × 30) | adds        | +$8.40          |

- **What each variable does to the prediction:**
    - One additional person in **population** is associated with $**0.0015** more in the predicted rate.
    - One additional **accident per 1,000 people** is associated with $**2.35** more.
    - A one-point improvement in **road quality** is associated with $**1.78** less.
    - A one-point increase in **crime rate** is associated with $**0.97** more.
    - A one-point improvement in **weather patterns** is associated with $**0.045** less.
    - A one-point increase in **traffic congestion** is associated with $**0.215** more.
    - One additional minute of **average commute time** is associated with $**0.28** more.

# [[Linear regression]] specifics
- Linear regression table provides:
    - [[Coefficient]]: The estimated difference in the outcome variable for a one-unit increase in a factor variable, while the other factor variables remain the same. For example, one additional accident per 1,000 people is associated with a $2.35 higher predicted insurance rate.
        - [[Standard error]]: A measure of uncertainty in an estimated coefficient. Smaller standard errors generally mean more precise estimates. Standard errors appear in parentheses beneath the coefficients.
    - [[Standardized coefficient]]: The coefficient is rescaled to allow comparison across factor variables measured in different units. The larger its absolute value, the stronger its association with the outcome variable in the model. Standard errors appear in parentheses below the standardized coefficients.
        - This is useful because different variables are measured differently.
            - Age is measured in years.
            - Education is measured in years.
            - Income may be measured in dollars.
            - Occupational prestige may be measured with a score.
    - [[p-value]]: Helps us assess evidence against the starting assumption that a factor has no association with the outcome, after accounting for the other factors in the model. When the p-value is less than 0.05 (p < 0.05), we call that coefficient statistically significant.
    - [[Adjusted R-squared]]: Shows how much of the variation in the outcome variable is explained by the regression model. It also helps us see whether adding more factor variables improves the model. We report adjusted R-squared as a percentage.

### The presence of [[confounding variable]]
- **What changed?**
    - In earlier models, population density of residence during adolescence years was statistically significantly associated with personal income.
        - After adding education, its coefficient became smaller and its p-value became greater than 0.05 (p > 0.05).
- **What does this mean?**
    - Some of the relationship between population density of residence during adolescence years and income may overlap with the relationship between education and income.
        - This may help explain why the coefficient changes when we account for education. However, these results alone do not establish why the change happened. The number of observations also changes across models, which can affect the estimates.
- **What is a confounding variable?**
    - A confounding variable is related to both the factor we are studying and the outcome. If we leave it out, the estimated relationship may be misleading.
        - For example, education may help us understand the relationship between population density of residence during adolescence years and income. We would need more evidence to determine whether it acts as a confounder in this study.
- **What does this mean, again?**
    - Our first model examined population density of residence during adolescence years without accounting for education.
        - Our final model also includes education, age, and occupational prestige. The population density of residence during adolescence years coefficient is smaller.
    - When the p-value is greater than 0.05 (p > 0.05), we do not have enough evidence to conclude that the population density of residence during adolescence years coefficient differs from zero in the final model.
        - This is why adding more relevant variables can help us better describe relationships in regression analysis.

    - **Is it?**

    - Education ranges from 0 to 20 years; that gives **21** possible whole-number values.
        - ```mermaid
        flowchart LR
            A1["0"] -->|$2,624| A2["1"] --> A3["..."] --> A4["12"] -->|$2,624| A5["13"] --> A6["..."] --> A7["20"]

            A1["16"] -->|$649| A2["17"] --> A3["..."] --> A4["66"] -->|$649| A5["67"] --> A6["..."] --> A7["80"]

            - Coefficients depend on the units used to measure each variable. A one-year difference in education is not the same kind of change as a one-point difference in occupational prestige.
            - If we measured parental income in dollars instead of thousands of dollars, its coefficient would look much smaller even though the relationship would be the same.
                - Therefore, raw coefficients should not be used to rank factors measured in different units.
                    - We **CAN’T** directly say "education has a stronger relationship with income than occupational prestige does" just because its raw coefficient is larger.

### [[Reporting of standardized coefficients]] (std. Coeff.)
- To compare the relative strength of factor variables, we use [[standardized coefficient]].
- Standardized coefficients put all variables on the same scale.
    - This way, we can compare variables even if they are measured differently and with different ranges.
        - Population density is measured with categories **(1-6)**.
        - Age is measured in years **(18-89)**.
        - Occupational prestige is measured with a prestige score **(16-80)**.
        - Education is measured in years **(0-20)**.
<!-- slide-break -->
- Therefore, standardized coefficients answer a slightly different question:
    - **Not** “How much does one unit increase income?”
    - **But** “Which factor has the largest standardized association with income in this model?”
- Based on standardized coefficients In the final model:
    - **"0.27" ➜ Occupational prestige score** has the largest standardized coefficient.
    - **"0.22" ➜ Education** has the second largest.
    - **"0.09" ➜ Age** has a smaller standardized coefficient.
    - **"0.02" ➜ Residence category** has the smallest standardized coefficient. When the p-value is greater than 0.05 (p > 0.05), it is not statistically significant.
<!-- slide-break -->
- This is why we should **not** compare coefficients directly.
    - Education has a larger raw coefficient than occupational prestige:
        - Education coefficient = **2,624**
        - Occupational prestige coefficient = **649**
    - But the standardized coefficient of occupational prestige is larger:
        - Occupational prestige std. Coeff. = **0.27**
        - Education std. Coeff. = **0.22**
    - This means that occupational prestige has a larger standardized association with personal income in this model.
<!-- slide-break -->
- We saw a similar issue in the **car insurance rate example**.
    - The coefficient for population was very small:
        - Population coefficient = **0.0015**
        - At first, this looks like population has almost no association with insurance rates.
            - However, the standardized coefficient for population was the highest:
                - Population std. Beta = **0.41**
    - This happens because population has a very large range.
        - A one-person difference corresponds to only a small difference in the predicted rate.
        - But a one-standard-deviation difference in city population can be large.
            - Therefore, a small raw coefficient can still correspond to a relatively large standardized association.
<!-- slide-break -->
- **In short**:
    - [[Coefficient]]s show the estimated association for a **one-unit increase** in a factor, after accounting for other factors.
    - [[Standardized coefficient]]s show the **relative size** of associations when factors are measured in different units.
        - To compare the sizes of these associations, we look at the absolute values of standardized coefficients, not raw coefficients.
        - In the final model, this is how we would rank the statistically significant factors by their standardized coefficients.
            - The largest standardized association with respondents’ personal income is for respondents' occupational prestige score **(std. Coeff=0.27)**, followed by respondents' education in years **(std. Coeff=0.22)**, and respondents' age **(std. Coeff=0.09)**.
<!-- slide-break -->
### [[Reporting of adjusted R-squared]] (R² adjusted)
- The [[adjusted R-squared]] value summarizes how much of the variation in the outcome is accounted for by the entire model, while adjusting for the number of factor variables.
    - It helps us compare models that include different numbers of factors.
    - The adjusted R-squared value should be reported as a percentage.
- Here's a shortcut for converting a number with decimals to a percentage:
    - Move the dot two times to the right:
        - 0.004 ➜ 0.4%
        - 0.023 ➜ 2.3%
        - 0.159 ➜ 15.9%
        - 0.196 ➜ 19.6%
- In our regression models:
    - Model 1 adjusted R-squared: **0.004** ➜ **0.4%**
    - Model 2 adjusted R-squared: **0.023** ➜ **2.3%**
    - Model 3 adjusted R-squared: **0.159** ➜ **15.9%**
    - Model 4 adjusted R-squared: **0.196** ➜ **19.6%**
<!-- slide-break -->
- This means that every additional factor variable improved the subsequent regression models.
    - Model 2 explains more of personal income than Model 1.
    - Model 3 explains more of personal income than Model 2.
    - Model 4 explains more of personal income than Model 3.
- In the final model, the adjusted R-squared is **0.196**.
    - This means that population density of residence during adolescence years, age, occupational prestige score, and education **account for 19.6%** of the variation in respondents' personal income in this model.
    - The remaining variation (**80.4%**) is not accounted for by this model. It may involve other factors, measurement differences, or chance.
